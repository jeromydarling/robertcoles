The Masterwork · 1967 — 1977

Children of Crisis

Five volumes. Seventeen years of fieldwork. Thousands of children across a changing nation. Two of these books shared the 1973 Pulitzer Prize — and together they remain among the most humane documents America has produced about itself.

It began as one book and grew, almost helplessly, into five. Coles had set out only to write up what he had seen in the desegregating South. But the families he followed kept moving — north to the cities, into new kinds of trouble — and his attention kept following them, until the project had become nothing less than a portrait of childhood across the whole American landscape: poor and rich, Black and white, migrant and settled, Native and newcomer.

What binds the volumes is not a thesis but a stance. Coles refused to reduce children to symptoms or statistics. He let them speak at length, reproduced their words and their drawings, and trusted the reader to feel the moral weight of a life. The result reads less like social science than like literature — Tolstoy with a tape recorder, one critic said — and it permanently enlarged what the country was willing to see.

Where it started: Ruby Bridges. In November 1960, six-year-old Ruby Bridges integrated New Orleans's William Frantz Elementary, walking each day through a mob held back by federal marshals. White families pulled their children out; for a time she was taught alone. Coles, then an Air Force psychiatrist, began visiting her at home. He expected to find a traumatized child. Instead he found one who told him she prayed for the people screaming at her — "because they don't know what they're doing." That contradiction between his training and the child in front of him is the hinge on which his entire life's work turned.

The Five Volumes

A reader's guide

I
1967

Children of Crisis

A Study of Courage and Fear

The foundation. Drawn from Coles's years in the desegregating South, the first volume follows Black children who pioneered the integration of white schools — and the white children, and adults, on the other side of the door. He refuses the easy melodrama of heroes and villains, attending instead to the daily psychological labor of children asked to carry a historical burden far heavier than their years.

Out of Ruby Bridges and her classmates, Coles builds a meditation on how ordinary people summon courage, and how fear works on the human spirit. It established both his subject and his unmistakable voice: patient, unhurried, morally awake.

II
1971
Pulitzer Prize, 1973

Migrants, Sharecroppers, Mountaineers

Volume II of Children of Crisis

Coles turns to the rural poor of the American South and Appalachia — the migrant farmworkers who follow the harvest with no place to call home, the sharecroppers bound to land they will never own, the mountain families of the hollows. He records how poverty shapes a child's sense of the possible: what such children expect from the world, and how little.

And yet the volume is no catalogue of misery. Coles is alert to the resilience, the inherited dignity, the religious depth of these families. It was one of the two volumes that shared the 1973 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction.

III
1972
Pulitzer Prize, 1973

The South Goes North

Volume III of Children of Crisis

The great migration, told from the inside. Coles follows the families of the first two volumes — Black and white Southerners alike — as they leave the countryside for the industrial cities of the North in search of work and dignity, and finds them confronting a new and colder kind of hardship: the ghetto, the housing project, the indifference of the city.

The promised land disappoints, and Coles records the disillusion without despair, tracing how children make sense of uprooting and arrival. The companion to Volume II, it completed the Pulitzer-winning pair and cemented his national reputation.

IV
1977

Eskimos, Chicanos, Indians

Volume IV of Children of Crisis

Coles widens the lens to the children of peoples too often left out of the national story altogether: Native American children on reservations, Inuit and Alaska Native children of the far north, and Mexican-American children of the Southwest. He listens for how heritage, land, and language anchor a child's identity — and what is lost when the dominant culture presses in.

Here his attention to landscape and the sacred deepens. The Hopi child's sense of the mesa, the Inuit child's reading of ice and weather — Coles treats these not as folklore but as serious ways of knowing the world.

V
1977

Privileged Ones

The Well-Off and the Rich in America · Volume V

The most surprising — and, to some readers, the most quietly devastating — volume. Having spent a decade among the poor, Coles trains the same unflinching attention on the children of wealth. He gives the phenomenon a name that entered the language: entitlement — the deep, often unspoken assumption of privileged children that the world is arranged for their benefit.

By refusing to exempt the comfortable from scrutiny, Coles closes the series with a moral symmetry: every child, rich or poor, is shaped by a circumstance not chosen, and every child deserves to be understood from the inside. It is the quiet argument of the whole work, made complete.

I have learned from these children to be careful about generalizations, and to remember that each child is a particular person, with a particular life to lead. Robert Coles · Children of Crisis

A companion shelf

Women of Crisis

The men's work had its necessary counterpart. With Jane Coles, his wife and collaborator, Coles drew on the same years of fieldwork to write two further volumes centering the women whose voices the original series had carried but not foregrounded — Women of Crisis: Lives of Struggle and Hope (1978) and Women of Crisis II: Lives of Work and Dreams (1980). Together with the five-volume sequence, they form the full architecture of the project the Coleses spent two decades building.
