Robert Coles

Physician of the listening kind

For more than half a century he sat on the floors of America's living rooms — in sharecroppers' cabins, in housing projects, on Hopi mesas, in the mansions of the rich — and asked children what they saw, what they feared, and what they hoped. Then he wrote it down, with such patience and grace that the country could not look away.

In memoriam

The most influential listener in America

Robert Coles, the child psychiatrist who turned the quiet testimony of ordinary children into one of the great documentary achievements of the twentieth century, died on June 4, 2026, at a hospice center in Lincoln, Massachusetts. He was 97.

He was the author of more than fifty books. He won the Pulitzer Prize in 1973 for two volumes of his five-volume Children of Crisis — a work begun by accident in 1960, when a young Air Force physician in New Orleans happened to see a six-year-old girl named Ruby Bridges walk through a screaming mob to integrate an all-white school. He could not forget her. He spent the rest of his life making sure the rest of us would not forget the children like her, either.

Time magazine, putting him on its cover in 1972, called him "the most influential living psychiatrist in the United States." Yet he distrusted the word psychiatrist almost as much as he distrusted theory. He preferred to think of himself as a witness — someone whose job was to pay attention, faithfully and humbly, to lives the powerful overlooked.

What children can teach us, if we will but listen, is the moral seriousness with which they take their own lives. Robert Coles
