The Method

The Art of Listening

No clipboard. No diagnosis at the door. Only crayons, paper, time, and the willingness to come back next week, and the week after that, for years.

Coles's method was, on the surface, almost nothing — and that was the point. He rejected the apparatus of his profession: the structured interview, the diagnostic checklist, the one-way mirror. He believed those tools put the observer in charge and turned the child into a specimen. What he offered instead was presence.

He went to the children's own homes and kitchens, not to a clinic. He visited the same family week after week, month after month, until he was no longer a stranger and the performance of being studied fell away. He did not lead with questions; he waited. He learned, as William Carlos Williams had taught him, that the way to know people is to keep showing up.

Children are not "cases." They are people, with a life to live and a story to tell — if only someone will sit still long enough to hear it. In the spirit of Robert Coles

Four habits

How he actually worked

Return, and return again

Patience over the interview

A single visit yields a pose; the tenth yields the truth. Coles measured his studies in years, not sessions, letting trust replace technique. Many of the children of Children of Crisis he followed for the better part of a decade.

Crayons and paper

Drawings as evidence

He asked children to draw — themselves, their families, their schools, the sky — and read the pictures as carefully as words. What a child enlarged, omitted, colored, or left blank told him what no questionnaire could.

The verbatim record

Their words, not his

His books are full of long, uninterrupted quotation. He resisted the urge to translate children into clinical language, trusting their own speech to carry meaning the expert's summary would flatten.

The witness's humility

Suspicion of theory

Trained in psychoanalysis, he came to distrust grand systems that explained children in advance. He let the particular child unsettle the general idea — and reported the contradiction rather than resolving it away.

Why it mattered

A documentary conscience

Coles called the resulting genre "documentary work," and he meant the word in its fullest sense — a discipline of fidelity to the real lives of real people. It sat at the crossroads of psychiatry, anthropology, journalism, and literature, and belonged fully to none of them. That was its strength. Freed from any single field's defenses, it could simply tell the truth about a child.

He carried the method outward for the rest of his life: into the documentary studies program he helped lead at Duke, into DoubleTake magazine, into a Harvard classroom where he taught a generation that attention itself is a moral act. To listen well, he insisted, is already to honor another person — and a society that learns to listen to its children is a society beginning to take them seriously.

The most important questions are the ones a child asks — and the most important answers are the ones we are humble enough to admit we do not have. In the spirit of Robert Coles
